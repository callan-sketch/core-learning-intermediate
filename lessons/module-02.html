Module 02 — Intermediate

Athlete Assessment & Data Interpretation

Written Guide + Quiz

Why It Matters

Assessment turns opinion into evidence. Without a systematic testing battery you are coaching based on what you think is true about an athlete. With one, you are coaching based on what you can measure, track, and prove. Data-driven coaching also gives athletes something concrete — numbers that move over time, evidence that the work is working. That is enormously motivating, and it builds trust in your programme.

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the rationale and protocols for each component of a climbing-specific testing battery
  • Conduct standardised assessments for finger strength, pulling strength, antagonist strength, endurance, mobility, and anthropometrics
  • Interpret results relative to climbing grade and limiter profiles
  • Identify an athlete's primary performance limiters vs. secondary compensations
  • Track change over time and determine whether adaptations are meaningful
  • Write a clear, actionable athlete assessment report

Section 1: Why Assess?

Athlete assessment serves four distinct purposes in climbing coaching:

  1. Baseline profiling — establishing where the athlete is now across all physical qualities
  2. Limiter identification — determining which qualities are restricting performance relative to the athlete's current grade
  3. Programme targeting — prioritising training resources toward the qualities that will generate the greatest performance return
  4. Progress tracking — confirming that the programme is producing the intended adaptations

Important Distinction

A limiter is a quality that, if improved, would directly improve climbing performance. A weakness is simply a quality below average. Not all weaknesses are limiters. A climber with below-average shoulder mobility may still be climbing well — their shoulder mobility is not currently limiting them. Focusing on non-limiting weaknesses is one of the most common errors in athlete programme design.

1.1 Assessment Principles

  • Standardise everything. Time of day, warm-up protocol, instructions, rest periods, equipment, order of tests. If conditions vary between tests, the data is not comparable.
  • Assess fresh, not fatigued. Schedule testing at least 48 hours after any significant climbing or training load.
  • Warm up consistently. A standardised 10-minute warm-up before every testing session.
  • Record conditions. Temperature, equipment used, any athlete notes (poor sleep, minor injury). These contextualise outliers.
  • Test before prescribing. Never design a programme without first knowing where the athlete starts.

Section 2: The Testing Battery

2.1 Anthropometrics

Anthropometric measures provide context for all other data. They do not drive programme decisions directly but help interpret performance relative to body dimensions.

2.2 Finger Strength

Finger strength is the most strongly correlated individual physical quality with climbing performance at all levels above beginner. It is non-negotiable to assess.

Maximum Hang (Relative Finger Strength)

Protocol: 20mm edge (or 18mm for advanced athletes), half-crimp position, 10-second hang, body weight plus added load. Result expressed as % body weight (body weight + added load, divided by body weight × 100).

  • Warm up thoroughly — 3-4 progressively heavier warm-up hangs before testing
  • Test both hands separately and combined
  • Record the maximum load at which the athlete can complete the full 10 seconds with correct form
  • Asymmetry >10% between hands warrants attention in programme design

Force-Endurance (Repeaters Protocol)

Protocol: 7 seconds on, 3 seconds off × 6 repetitions = 1 set. Load = 60% of maximum hang. Record total time before failure or significant form breakdown. Alternatively, use a Tindeq or similar force gauge to capture a force-time curve across the set.

Equipment Note

A Tindeq Progressor or similar isometric force gauge dramatically improves the quality of finger strength data. It allows you to capture peak force, rate of force development, and endurance curves rather than simply pass/fail on a timed hang. If you have access to one, integrate it into the battery from the start.

2.6 Climbing-Specific Endurance

ARC Test (Aerobic Restoration and Capillarisation): Continuous climbing at an easy grade (40-50% effort, conversational pace) for 20-30 minutes without stopping. Record whether the athlete can maintain this, note grade used. A well-trained aerobic base allows this comfortably on a grade 2-3 numbers below maximum.

Boulder Problem Repeats: Repeat a moderate problem (70-75% of max grade) with 2:1 work:rest ratio for 20 minutes. Track how many successful completions occur in each 5-minute block. A declining completion rate across blocks indicates poor endurance relative to current strength level.

3.1 Relative Strength Benchmarks by Grade

3.2 Identifying Limiters vs. Compensations

When reading a full assessment profile, look for the gap — the quality that is significantly below what you would expect for the athlete's current climbing level. That gap is your primary coaching target.

Common limiter patterns in intermediate climbers:

  • High technique, low finger strength — climber exceeds grade expectations on technique-dependent routes, fails on steep power problems. Training target: systematic fingerboard work.
  • High strength, poor endurance — climber crushes moves but cannot string a route together, falls pumped. Training target: aerobic base and force-endurance work.
  • Good upper body, poor hip mobility — climber avoids hip-turn and drop-knee moves, appears stiff and rigid on the wall. Training target: hip mobility and movement pattern drilling.
  • Good physical profile, underperforms on grade — no single physical limiter. This is a signal that technique, tactical, or psychological factors are restricting performance.

3.3 Tracking Change Over Time

Re-test every 8-12 weeks, depending on training block length. When comparing data points:

  • Changes in max hang of <3% are likely within measurement error — not meaningful
  • Changes of 5%+ are considered training adaptations worth noting
  • Always look at the full profile, not single metrics in isolation
  • Log any contextual notes that could affect comparability (new edge, different time of day, minor illness)

Section 4: Writing the Athlete Assessment Report

An assessment report is a coaching tool, not a scientific paper. It should give the athlete clarity, motivation, and direction. Structure it as follows:

  1. Athlete profile summary — name, date, current grade, years climbing, training history, goals
  2. Test results table — all measures recorded, with comparison to previous test date if applicable
  3. Strength summary — what the athlete does well, relative to their grade and goals
  4. Primary limiter — the one quality most restricting performance progression
  5. Secondary targets — 1-2 other areas worth attention over the medium term
  6. Programme recommendations — specific, concrete, and directly derived from the data
  7. Next re-test date — scheduled, not vague

Report Writing Tip

Write the report for the athlete, not for yourself. Avoid jargon unless you define it. Lead with what they are good at before discussing what needs work. Frame limiters as development opportunities, not deficits. The athlete should leave the report conversation feeling clear about where to focus and confident that their coach has a plan.

Module Summary

Athlete Assessment & Data Interpretation

Key Takeaways

  • Assessment turns coaching opinion into evidence. Without it, you are guessing what an athlete needs.
  • Standardise everything — conditions, protocols, equipment, timing. Inconsistency makes data incomparable.
  • The full battery covers finger strength, pulling strength, antagonist balance, mobility, anthropometrics, and endurance. No single metric tells the full story.
  • Limiter identification — finding the gap — is the most important output of an assessment. Not all weaknesses are limiters.
  • Benchmarks by grade give directional guidance, not rigid targets. Individual profiles vary substantially.
  • An athlete report should give clarity and direction, not overwhelm. Lead with strengths, one primary limiter, concrete recommendations.
