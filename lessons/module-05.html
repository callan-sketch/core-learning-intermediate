Module 05 — Intermediate

Coaching Communication & Athlete Management

Written Guide + Quiz

Why It Matters

Technical knowledge is necessary but not sufficient. The most precisely designed programme fails if the athlete does not understand it, does not trust it, or does not feel heard by the coach prescribing it. How you communicate — the words you choose, the questions you ask, the conversations you're willing to have — determines whether your athletes stay, improve, and develop a healthy relationship with their sport and their coach.

Learning Objectives

  • Give effective performance feedback using established frameworks
  • Select and apply the right cueing language for different athletes and situations
  • Manage the athlete-coach relationship across different stages and contexts
  • Conduct goal-setting conversations that are genuinely useful
  • Navigate plateau management, injury conversations, and other difficult discussions
  • Manage groups effectively alongside individual athletes

Section 1: Giving Effective Feedback

1.1 Feedback Frameworks

Unstructured feedback tends to be inconsistent, disproportionately negative (we notice what goes wrong), and difficult for athletes to act on. Frameworks provide structure without making feedback feel scripted.

The SBI Model (Situation–Behaviour–Impact)

Describe the specific situation (when, where, what move), the observable behaviour (what you saw), and its impact (what happened as a result). Avoid inference or judgment about why.

Example: 'On the third clip of that route [situation], your left foot drifted off the foothold before you reached for the next draw [behaviour], which left you with no base support and you barn-doored off [impact].' This is more usable than 'your footwork let you down on that route.'

Ask First

Before giving feedback, ask what the athlete noticed. 'What happened at that move?' 'What did that feel like?' This activates self-monitoring, tells you what the athlete already knows, and prevents you from repeating what they already understand. When an athlete correctly identifies their own error, affirm it and ask what they want to try next — that is better than any feedback you could give.

1.2 Feedback Timing

  • Concurrent feedback (during the movement) — almost never useful in climbing. Calling cues while someone is on the wall splits attention and usually helps neither the movement nor the learning. The exception: calling out holds in a situation where the athlete cannot see the next move.
  • Terminal feedback (immediately after) — useful for safety issues, corrections to prevent injury pattern repetition, or when the athlete asks. Otherwise, give them a moment first.
  • Delayed feedback (some minutes after, or at review) — often produces better learning, especially for less explicit corrections. 'When you review the video later, notice your hip position on the crux' versus correcting immediately.

1.3 The Positive:Corrective Ratio

Research in sport coaching consistently shows that coaches underestimate the proportion of negative/corrective feedback they give. Aim for a ratio of approximately 3:1 (positive or instructional to corrective). This is not about false praise — it is about making the feedback environment feel safe enough for athletes to take risks, make mistakes, and try new things without fear of constant criticism.

Practical Test

Record audio of one of your sessions and count the ratio of positive/neutral to corrective statements. Most coaches discover a significant gap between what they think they do and what they actually do. The data is usually more corrective-heavy than expected. Awareness is the first step.

Section 2: Cueing Language in Depth

2.1 Internal vs. External Cues

As established in Module 01, external cues (attention directed to effect or environment) produce better movement learning than internal cues (attention directed to body parts). In coaching practice, this means framing cues in terms of what the athlete is trying to do to something, not what they are doing with their body.

Internal CueExternal AlternativeWhy It's Better
'Bend your knees more''Press into that foothold harder'Focusing on the hold generates the knee bend naturally
'Keep your elbow in''Pull the hold into your hip'Direction of pull achieves elbow position without conscious thought
'Rotate your hip''Touch your hip to the wall'Tactile goal produces the rotation
'Don't look down''Find your next foothold with your eyes before you move'Gives a positive action rather than a prohibition

2.2 Analogies in Coaching

Analogy instruction — describing movement in terms of something the athlete already knows — promotes implicit learning and reduces conscious movement monitoring. Effective analogies share the essential feature of the target movement without describing the body parts:

  • 'Move like you're climbing a ladder' → upright body position, weight over feet
  • 'Like standing up from a chair' → driving through the legs on a high footstep
  • 'Like pushing a shopping trolley' → maintaining contact with the wall during a traverse
  • 'Imagine the wall is a window you don't want to leave fingerprints on' → open-hand grip, gentle contact

Test an analogy by asking whether a person with no climbing knowledge could follow the instruction and produce approximately the right movement. If yes, it's a good analogy.

2.3 Individual Cueing Preferences

Not all athletes respond to the same type of cue. Some are strongly visual (they respond to demonstration or seeing their own video). Some are kinaesthetic (they need to feel the movement). Some are auditory (they respond best to verbal instruction). Some prefer positive framing ('do this'), others respond better to negative framing ('avoid this'). Over time, learn which approach produces the fastest response in each athlete. Record it somewhere if you coach multiple athletes.

Section 3: Managing the Athlete-Coach Relationship

3.1 Autonomy vs. Direction

The appropriate level of coach direction varies by athlete experience, personality, and training context. Beginning coaches often default to high direction because it feels productive. Research suggests that athletes in high-autonomy coaching environments (where they have input into decisions, can ask questions, and are given reasons) show better long-term adherence and intrinsic motivation. High-direction coaching may produce faster short-term results but poorer long-term development.

In practice:

  • Give the athlete the rationale for what you are asking them to do
  • Invite questions and uncertainty — 'Does this make sense to you? Any questions about why?'
  • Where possible, give choices: 'We could do 4×4s or volume repeaters today — which feels right given how you slept?'
  • Allow athletes to express disagreement with your approach — and take it seriously

3.2 Goal-Setting Conversations

Effective goal-setting is a conversation, not a form. The SMART framework (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) is a useful structure but not a substitute for genuinely understanding what an athlete wants and why they want it.

Questions worth asking in a goal-setting conversation:

  • 'What does climbing give you that nothing else does?'
  • 'What would success look like for you in six months? What about five years?'
  • 'What is getting in the way most right now?'
  • 'Is there anything you're afraid of that we haven't talked about?'
  • 'What do you need from me as your coach?'

These questions often reveal more than 'what grade do you want to climb?' The answers should inform both programme design and how you manage the relationship.

3.3 When to Push and When to Hold Back

One of the hardest coaching judgments is knowing when to increase challenge and when to reduce it. Some signals:

SignalLikely MeaningResponse
Athlete is physically underperforming relative to recent baseline, appears flatAccumulated fatigue or life stressReduce load; check in on life outside climbing
Athlete avoids certain move types or settingsFear or confidence issue, not physical limitationGradual exposure, process focus, psychological support
Athlete pushes through pain or refuses to report sorenessFear of being held back; culture of toughness over healthCreate a safe environment for honesty; explain the injury costs of non-disclosure
Athlete is enthusiastic, energetic, wants moreMay be appropriate to increase load — or may be overconfidence after a good weekCheck the ACWR before increasing; enthusiasm is not a training load metric
Athlete regularly misses sessions, loses motivationProgramme-life mismatch, burnout signal, or relationship issueOpen conversation; revisit goals; consider reducing frequency not just intensity

3.4 Difficult Conversations

The conversations coaches avoid are often the most important ones. Common difficult conversations in climbing coaching:

  • Plateau talk: 'You haven't progressed in grade in six months — let's talk about why.' Be direct, data-driven, and solution-oriented. Avoid false reassurance.
  • Injury response: 'You need to stop training the way you've been training, or this will become a serious injury.' Be clear and firm. Athletes often need explicit medical-sounding framing to take injury seriously.
  • Expectation mismatch: 'I think your grade target for this season is too ambitious given your starting point. Here's what I think is realistic and why.' This requires courage. Saying nothing avoids discomfort now and creates disappointment later.
  • Programme non-compliance: 'I notice you've missed three sessions in the last two weeks. Is everything okay?' Ask before assuming. Life happens.

Section 4: Group Coaching Dynamics

4.1 Managing Individuals in a Group

Group sessions create a tension between group efficiency and individual needs. Strategies for managing this:

  • Know each athlete's individual limiter profile — not every athlete does the same drill for the same reason
  • Brief the group together, then give individual targets ('today we're doing 4×4s — for you, focus on foot precision; for you, focus on staying off your arms on the rests')
  • Use peer observation — athletes watching each other reduces the burden on the coach and builds self-monitoring skills
  • Check in privately during sessions with athletes who you know are struggling with something they may not raise in front of the group

4.2 Group Dynamics and Culture

The coaching culture you create in a group is set by what you model, what you tolerate, and what you reward. Groups develop norms — shared expectations about how members behave. Healthy group norms in a climbing team:

  • It is safe to try things and fail
  • Injury and soreness are reported honestly
  • Athletes support rather than compete with each other in training
  • Questions are welcomed, not seen as challenging the coach
  • Progress is celebrated beyond grade numbers alone

Unhealthy norms — excessive grade-focus, athletes pushing through injury to appear tough, fear of asking questions — are usually a reflection of the coach's own behaviour, whether the coach realises it or not.

Module Summary

Coaching Communication & Athlete Management

Key Takeaways

  • Effective feedback is specific, observable, and timed appropriately. The SBI model gives you structure without making feedback feel scripted.
  • Ask before you tell. The athlete's self-assessment is data — and acting on it produces better learning than correction alone.
  • External cues beat internal cues. Direct attention to effect, not body parts.
  • Autonomy-supportive coaching produces better long-term adherence and intrinsic motivation than directive coaching. Give reasons; invite questions.
  • Goal-setting conversations reveal more than grade targets. Ask about what climbing gives the athlete, not just what they want to achieve.
  • The coaching culture you create is set by what you model and what you tolerate. Groups reflect their coaches.
